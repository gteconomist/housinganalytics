WAHI ships as a third income anchor — alongside HUD Area Median Income and local MHI — on every geography in the system. It is not a filter, a threshold, or an exception flag. Every screened geography gets all three numbers; nothing is suppressed and nothing is singled out for review based on how large the ratio is.

The rest of this brief is the evidence for that decision, and a short account of why the opposite decision — an exception flag firing at high ratios — was recommended first and then abandoned.

02 The distortion is universal, not exceptional

Working-age households out-earn all households in essentially every American community. The median WAHI/MHI ratio is 1.16× — a 16% premium — and it holds across the 5,132 geographies that clear the reliability screen.

A fixed 1.15 threshold would fire in 54.2% of geographies. That is not a signal, it is the base rate. A condition that fires in more than half of all cases carries no information about which of those cases is unusual.

03 Why it is not a flag — the Muscogee test

Muscogee County, Georgia sits at the 25th percentile of distortion — ratio 1.109× against the 1.157× norm. Ordinary. Nothing an exception flag would catch.

Yet the three anchors disagree by a lot: HUD AMI puts the gap at +$3,193, local MHI at +$325, WAHI at +$1,974. An analyst reading only MHI would call Muscogee close to balanced. An analyst reading WAHI would call it a meaningful shortage. Both are looking at the same county.

04 A note on units

The Muscogee move reads as 507% if you express it as a percentage of the MHI-anchored gap (+$1,649 on a base of $325) — because that base is near zero. Percentages of a near-zero denominator are unstable and misleading.

Correctly stated, the move is +$1,649 units on 40,025 renter households, or 41 per 1,000 — the 78th percentile nationally (national median 15 per 1,000). All reporting in this system uses units per 1,000 renter households. Percentages of the MHI gap are reported nowhere, because that gap approaches zero in exactly the geographies where the anchor choice matters most.

05 It is not a military artifact

Muscogee is genuinely military-heavy — armed forces are 6.86% of the labor force, the 97.9th percentile nationally — and Fort Moore sits inside the county. The objection that WAHI is picking up a base-town artifact was well-founded and had to be tested.

It does not survive the test. Military presence pushes the ratio the other way: corr(armed-forces share, ratio) = -0.109. Its correlation with the size of the gap move is negligible: +0.029.

06 What drives it

Senior-headed households are a median 28.7% of all households and earn a median 63% of what working-age households earn. Pulling seniors out of the household-income denominator mechanically raises the median wherever they are present in volume.

A composite driver — senior share × senior income deficit, plus a student term — correlates 0.83 with the ratio itself. The measure behaves exactly as theory predicts: it is not noise, it is the compositional effect of household age structure on median household income, measured and reproduced.

07 The tails, as reporting colour not as a trigger

The tails are real and worth publishing — they are just not a rule. High-ratio places are college towns and retirement communities. Low-ratio places are distressed post-industrial cities where working-age households earn no premium at all over the household median — a separate and equally publishable signal, running the opposite direction from the exception-flag framing.

08 WAHI reconciles the other two anchors

Where MHI and AMI disagree by more than 50 units (n = 3,695), WAHI closes a median 38.9% of the disagreement, and lands closer to the AMI answer than MHI does in 65.9% of geographies.

The interpretation: part of why HUD AMI runs above local MHI is not HUD's regional, family-size, and four-person adjustments — it is that MHI counts retirees and students, while the workforce band is meant to describe workers. WAHI removes that mismatch directly. Muscogee illustrates the reconciliation in one line: +$325 → +$1,974 → +$3,193.

09 What WAHI is — and is not

Definition

WAHI is the median income of households headed by someone aged 25–64, drawn from ACS table B19037 (age of householder by household income).

It is not the income of working-age adults. ACS classifies an entire household by the age of one reference person: a 70-year-old still working is excluded if a younger person is not the householder, and a retired parent living in a 45-year-old's household is counted in full. Read WAHI as “what households run by working-age people earn here,” not as a measure of individual worker earnings.

Two naming notes, on the record. The name AWI was rejected: it collides with the Social Security Administration's Average Wage Index, and “adjusted” implies an adjustment factor that does not exist here — this is a restricted median, not an adjusted one. And 25–64 is working age, not prime working age, which by BLS convention is 25–54. The two terms are not interchangeable and the brief uses only the former.

10 Method and validation

Interpolation. B19037 publishes household counts by income bracket separately for the 25–44 and 45–64 householder-age cohorts. The two cohorts are pooled bracket-by-bracket into a single 25–64 distribution, and the median is found by linear interpolation inside the bracket that contains it. The open top bracket is modelled as a $200,000–$400,000 range and geographies whose derived median falls inside it are flagged rather than silently reported.

The same interpolation, by the same method, produces the MHI denominator used in the WAHI/MHI ratio. Grouped-data interpolation error is not eliminated, but because both numerator and denominator are built the same way from the same table, that error cancels in the ratio far more than it would in either figure taken alone.

  1. Between-cohorts constraint (100.00%). The pooled 25–64 median lies between the published 25–44 and 45–64 cohort medians in every screened geography — a hard mathematical constraint the method must satisfy, and does, without exception.
  2. Port fidelity (8,034 / 8,034). The workforce-band math was ported to Python and reproduces the production build's committed field exactly in all geographies.
  3. Interpolation accuracy (+0.19% median error). The same method applied to the full distribution reproduces published B19013 median household income with median error +0.19% (p5 -3.5%, p95 +4.2%).
  4. End-to-end. STATES=13 run of the production build script reproduced the prototype exactly for Muscogee (wahi 64,421 · ratio 1.109 · +3,193 / +325 / +1,974 · 41.2 per 1,000); 296/296 Georgia geographies received all three anchors
  5. Syntax. node --check on both JS files; @astrojs/compiler on HousingGapCard.astro — 0 diagnostics

Reliability screen: ≥1,000 households, ≥500 working-age households, CV<15% on both cohort medians, derived median not in the open top bracket — 5,132 of 8,034 geographies pass. The screen is display-only. WAHI is computed and shown on every geography regardless of screen status; geographies that fail it carry a thin-sample caution label rather than being hidden.

11 What changed and why

The first recommendation on this project was an exception flag: fire when the WAHI/MHI ratio reaches 1.30 or higher. Muscogee falsified it. Muscogee sits at the 25th percentile of distortion — ordinary, nowhere near 1.30, nothing a flag would catch — and the anchor choice still swings the workforce gap from +$325 to +$1,974.

Generalising the test across every ratio band (section 03) confirmed it was not a one-off: the sign-flip rate sits at roughly 10% in every band, including the least-distorted one. A trigger is supposed to separate cases that matter from cases that don't. A condition that fires at the same rate regardless of how extreme it looks is not measuring the thing it claims to measure.

This is the same failure mode as an earlier band-share trigger elsewhere on this project: a condition that fires on more than half of all cases, or fires uncorrelated with the outcome it's meant to predict, is not a trigger — it's noise with a threshold attached. The fix in both cases was the same: stop trying to flag the exception, and publish the number everywhere instead.
